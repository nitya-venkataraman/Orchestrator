Who this is for

The people the features below are meant to serve.

Marcus Vance, Managing Director, Private Credit

primary

Marcus runs large-scale corporate lending and opportunistic credit at a major alternative asset manager. He does not build analysis; he decides on it, and he decides under time pressure, because the market has moved toward privately negotiated, bespoke structures where the firm that can issue a term sheet first tends to win. His day is a stream of partial signals arriving from incompatible places: public filings, private databases, relationship logs, macro feeds, and his own team's memory. Once a deal is booked he switches from offense to defense and inherits a monitoring regime he openly distrusts, because it runs on quarterly covenant submissions that borrowers type into spreadsheets. He describes his current posture as reactive and says that by the time a breach shows up in that data he is three months too late to structure a workout. He is allergic to dashboards with fifty flashing lights and evaluates any new tool on two axes only: does it tell him something he can act on today, and does the firm's proprietary data stay inside the firm's own perimeter.

Goals

  • Compress initial vetting of a mid-market borrower from roughly three days to something closer to thirty minutes
  • Issue term sheets fast enough to win competitive, privately negotiated deals
  • Know what his own firm already knows about a target before anyone spends an hour on it
  • See credit deterioration while there is still time to restructure or refinance, not after the breach
  • Receive a small number of alerts that each state the issue, the firm's dollar exposure, and the exact distance to a covenant trip
  • Keep proprietary deal data isolated inside the firm's own cloud environment

Frustrations

  • Data he needs is scattered across filings, private databases, relationship logs, and macro feeds with no common entry point
  • Prior work by other teams sits in legacy PDFs on internal drives or inside an individual's Outlook inbox and is effectively lost to the firm
  • Associates burn 48 to 72 hours building a foundational profile before he can form a view
  • Quarterly covenant reporting is a lagging indicator that surfaces problems only after the window to act has closed
  • Monitoring dashboards flood him with noise instead of a ranked, decision-ready feed
  • Any platform that requires his data to leave the firm's perimeter is a non-starter regardless of its capability

Alex Mercer, Investment and Portfolio Associate

primary

Alex is the person the 48-to-72-hour profile build actually happens to. Assignment triggers a familiar opening move: a tracking spreadsheet, then Capital IQ and Bloomberg to establish whether the entity is public or private, then the internal CRM, Salesforce, and the credit document repository to find out whether the firm has ever touched this client, its sponsors, or its subsidiaries. By Alex's own estimate the split is roughly seventy percent finding and formatting data against thirty percent actual analysis, which means the investment thesis, the leverage metrics, and the industry read get written late on the second night because the first day went entirely to data scrubbing. The single worst block is corporate structure: for a sponsor-backed private borrower, hunting the ultimate parent and mapping co-issuers and guarantors out of hundreds of pages of credit agreements and pitch books eats four to five hours and ends in a hand-drawn PowerPoint chart. Alex's other constant is dependency risk; an incomplete document set from the sponsor stops the model dead while the deadline keeps moving, and the fix is an email chain through the deal team that may take a day to come back. Alex describes the resulting role as a glorified data entry clerk and has one clear wish: an AI that reads the credit agreement and fills in the template.

Goals

  • Spend the majority of the build window on analysis rather than on locating and reformatting data
  • Produce a corporate structure and guarantee map without manually parsing credit agreements
  • Auto-populate the financial template with covenants, EBITDA definitions, and amortization schedules pulled from source documents
  • Deliver a profile the Credit Risk Officer accepts on the first pass
  • Keep the build progressing even when a sponsor document set arrives incomplete

Frustrations

  • Roughly seventy percent of the window goes to finding and formatting rather than analyzing
  • Four to five hours per deal disappear into manually building an org chart in PowerPoint
  • Private and mid-market entities are thinly covered by Capital IQ, forcing PDF-by-PDF reconstruction
  • Copying financials out of PDFs into an Excel template breaks formulas and invites formatting complaints
  • A missing cash flow statement or footnote set stops all progress while the clock keeps running
  • Thesis and risk work gets pushed to night two, when judgment is at its worst

Elena Rostova, Director of Credit Risk and Portfolio Operations

secondary

Elena is the gate between a finished profile and the credit committee, and she works in twenty to thirty minute passes. Her review is deliberately narrow: total leverage and exactly how adjusted EBITDA was built, particularly the add-backs; then liquidity runway in cash and revolver capacity; then corporate structure and the guarantee package, so she knows who is actually on the hook and whether unrated offshore subsidiaries are leaking cash. She skips company history entirely, because founding dates do not tell her what cash flow looks like over the next twenty-four months. What she catches most often is not sloppiness but misplaced trust: an associate accepting a Capital IQ pull without adjusting for pro-forma effects of a recent acquisition, or waving through a one-time consulting fee that has appeared in each of the last three years. A single error of that kind costs her confidence in the entire document, and then she has a choice between silently fixing it herself to avoid email ping-pong or sending it back and triggering a rush-rework loop that postpones her sign-off, postpones the credit committee, and puts a live transaction's closing date at risk. Her stated ideal is not a better PDF but a dynamic web view where clicking a leverage ratio shows the exact sentence in the source credit agreement it came from, which she believes would cut her review to five minutes.

Goals

  • Verify any figure against its source sentence without reopening a 300-page document
  • Reach a confident sign-off in as little as five minutes per profile
  • See add-backs, pro-forma adjustments, and their history surfaced rather than buried
  • Understand the guarantee package and identify cash leakage through unrated subsidiaries quickly
  • Protect the credit committee calendar and the closing date from avoidable rework

Frustrations

  • Static PDFs and Word documents make verification expensive, so trust becomes a judgment about the associate rather than the data
  • Associates inherit vendor data uncritically, leaving pro-forma and add-back errors for her to catch
  • One material error destroys her confidence in an otherwise well-built profile
  • Sending work back triggers a rush-rework loop that cascades into a postponed credit committee
  • Minor fixes fall to her because chasing them by email costs more time than doing them

Information Barrier Owner, Compliance and Platform Security

secondary

This persona is inferred from the product requirements document's security architecture section rather than from any interview, so their goals below are read off stated technical requirements and not off observed behavior. They own the conditions under which a cross-asset intelligence layer is allowed to exist at all. The platform's premise is that Private Equity, Credit, Infrastructure, and Real Estate teams can draw on one another's history, and this persona's job is to ensure that drawing on it never becomes an unmanaged flow of material non-public information between a private-side deal team and a public liquid strategies desk. Their instruments are single-tenant deployment inside the firm's own cloud, role-based access control driven by metadata tagging at the document level, and a hard requirement that foundational models run on local inference with no retention of prompts, search histories, or ingested documents. Marcus's comment that security is the precondition for adoption is the commercial expression of this persona's veto.

Goals

  • Keep the entire platform and all inference inside the firm's own cloud perimeter
  • Enforce document-level access control so private-side deal notes stay invisible to public-side teams unless explicitly permitted
  • Ensure prompts, search histories, and ingested documents are never retained or used for external model training
  • Allow firm-wide synthesis to proceed without creating a compliance or insider-trading exposure

Frustrations

  • A search layer that indexes everything is by default a system that leaks everything
  • Access decisions depend on metadata tagging quality across legacy repositories that were never tagged for this purpose
  • Generative answers can reconstruct restricted content from permitted fragments, which conventional access controls do not catch

Design directions

Different bets about how to solve the problem, not feature bundles. No direction is recommended here — that choice is yours.

Firm Memory

Build the product as the firm's institutional recall. One entity search is the front door to everything the organization has ever learned about a company, its sponsors, and its subsidiaries, drawn out of legacy repositories, mail archives, and the CRM and rendered as a relationship timeline with owners attached. Every later workflow, from screening to monitoring, hangs off that spine, and the system gets more valuable each time anyone uses it because their work becomes part of the record.

Differentiator

The asset is internal and unpurchasable. Competitors sell external market data that every rival firm can also buy; this bet makes the product a store of proprietary advantage that compounds with tenure and cannot be replicated by a competitor's subscription.

Tradeoff

It is worth almost nothing on day one. Value depends on ingesting a messy legacy corpus and on access-control tagging that nobody ever applied to those documents, so the deployment is long, the compliance surface is at its widest, and the benefit is a hard-to-attribute improvement in judgment rather than a number anyone can put in a renewal case.

The Verifiable Profile

Treat the deal profile itself as the product. Documents are read into a structured, live profile where every figure stays bound to its source sentence, weak assumptions are flagged on the face of the document, the math is re-derived before submission, and the reviewer interrogates a web workspace instead of a static file. The target is the whole path from assignment to sign-off, compressing both the build and the review loop it feeds.

Differentiator

It attacks the only cost in this research that anyone has actually quantified, and it is the one bet that serves the builder and the reviewer with the same artifact. Success is legible in days of associate time returned and in rework loops that no longer postpone a committee.

Tradeoff

It is an efficiency tool, not an investment edge, so it competes on cost savings rather than on alpha and is easier to defund. Extraction has to be near-perfect from the first week: one wrong leverage figure with a confident source link costs more trust than a hundred correct ones earn, and the reviewer's escape hatch of reopening the PDF is always one click away. The workspace also has to bend to each firm's templates and house conventions, which makes every deployment partly bespoke.

Early Warning Room

Aim the product at the portfolio rather than the pipeline. Alternative signals stream in daily against every booked credit, deviation from sector benchmarks drives a moving health score, and the interface a decision-maker actually touches is a short ranked feed in which each item carries the event, the dollar exposure, and the exact distance to a covenant trip. The promise is converting a lagging quarterly process into a leading one and buying back the months that currently separate a problem from its discovery.

Differentiator

It is the only direction that changes an outcome rather than a cost. A workout structured three months early is worth more than every hour of associate time in the other bets combined, and it is aimed straight at the person who signs the contract, which makes it the easiest to sell and the easiest to price on risk avoided.

Tradeoff

The whole bet rests on alternative signals predicting credit distress, which this research asserts but does not demonstrate, and the failure mode is asymmetric: a few false alarms and the MD who said he does not want fifty flashing lights stops opening the feed, after which nothing recovers it. It also carries recurring alternative-data cost, and when it works the result is a non-event, which is notoriously hard to claim credit for at renewal.
